# Ultimate Meal efficiency leaderboard — 2026-09-07

This round asks a narrower question than the retailer comparison:

> Which ordinary foods are doing the most useful nutritional work per Canadian dollar, and how low can a 3,000–3,500 kcal / high-protein / high-fibre day plausibly go?

All prices below are dated snapshots. Flyer prices move. Wholesale web prices can differ from in-store cash-and-carry prices.

Nutrition figures used for rough comparisons are package-label values where available and otherwise ordinary-food planning estimates. Recalculate Prototype 1 from the packages actually purchased.

### The funny result

Whey is still excellent for **protein with almost no cooking and little food volume**. It is mediocre on pure protein-per-dollar beside wholesale lentils and soy.

Greek yogurt / skyr has the same fate: excellent texture, calcium and convenience; expensive as the main budget-protein engine.

Olive oil also changes role. Bulk starches and pulses can beat it on calories per dollar. Oil stays because it adds calories with almost no food volume, which is valuable when bulking.

## Other weirdly strong finds

### Pasta deserves to challenge rice

Maxi currently has No Name pasta at **$2.00 / 900 g**. The label gives 310 kcal, 10 g protein and 4 g fibre per 85 g.

That makes pasta a very serious Ultimate Meal chassis: extremely cheap, more protein/fibre than white rice, and naturally compatible with a tomato + lentil + soy + chicken sauce.

### Potatoes become a sale-price micronutrient bomb

Food Basics currently has a 10 lb bag of white potatoes at **$1.88** through 2026-09-09.

At that flyer price they are absurdly cheap potassium-rich food. Their low calorie density makes them better as a micronutrient / carbohydrate module than as the entire bulking chassis.

### Peas beat generic mixed vegetables for some jobs

Walmart's 2 kg frozen green peas are **$6.48 (~$0.32/100 g)**. Peas contribute meaningful protein and fibre while still functioning as a frozen vegetable.

Use a smaller amount of spinach/carrot/pepper for micronutrient variety and let peas supply more of the cheap plant volume.

### Mackerel may beat sardines on pure dollars

A1 currently lists Mr. Goudas mackerel in tomato sauce at **$2.29 / 425 g**. The manufacturer's label is 160 kcal and 22 g protein per 142 g, so a whole can is roughly **480 kcal / 66 g protein**.

The label also lists calcium at 25% DV per 142 g serving. This makes the big can interesting as a rotating fish module. Sardines remain attractive in small cans and for their familiar bones/omega-3 role.

### Cacao nibs are fully demoted

For a mole-ish direction, cocoa powder is far cheaper than nibs. Use a small amount for flavour/polyphenol diversity if it earns its place; the meal does not need a heroic cacao dose.

### Rough planning envelope

Using common package/generic nutrition values, this draft lands around:

- **~3,400 kcal**
- **~220 g protein**
- **~59 g fibre**

## More practical price tiers

### Retail-only-ish

Use Walmart / Maxi / No Frills / Food Basics package sizes, including Walmart's ~$1.47/100 g Nutrela soy and ~$0.33/100 g lentils.

The same concept moves toward roughly **$8.5–9/day** instead of $7.5–8.

That is still far below the first Walmart-only v0 because the recipe itself has improved: less skyr, more cheap pulses/soy/milk, cheaper starches, and deliberately small expensive micronutrient modules.

### Small-wholesale sweet spot

Use A1's 5 lb soy and 10 lb red lentils, plus ordinary discount-banner shopping.

This captures most of the theoretical floor without buying restaurant quantities. Expected territory: **roughly $7.5–8.5/day**, depending on meat/berry/fish sales.

### Restaurant-supply goblin

20 kg soy + 50 lb lentils + 20 kg rice/pasta equivalents.

Useful as a mathematical lower bound. Completely unnecessary until the meal has survived many taste tests.

## Nutrient-job map for the price-floor draft

- **pasta / oats:** cheap energy, B vitamins/minerals, some protein/fibre
- **lentils:** protein, fibre, iron, folate, magnesium
- **textured soy:** concentrated complete-ish plant protein with low cost and low fat
- **chicken:** complete protein, taste, lower fibre/volume protein
- **milk:** calcium, B12, protein, calories, fortified vitamin D
- **eggs:** choline, protein, fat-soluble nutrients
- **mackerel:** fish protein, B12/selenium/fats; label also shows substantial calcium
- **flax:** ALA omega-3 + fibre
- **sunflower:** vitamin E + calories + minerals
- **peas / spinach / carrots / other vegetables:** vitamin A/C/K/folate and plant diversity
- **blueberries:** anthocyanin-rich fruit module
- **tomato:** lycopene + sauce coherence
- **iodized salt:** iodine
- **olive oil / peanut butter:** calorie-density tools that keep the food volume from becoming impossible

## Culinary implication

The economics are now pointing away from a burrito bowl as the only leading form.

A **giant tomato-based pasta / bolognese mutant** may be more coherent:

- pasta chassis
- red lentils cooked into the sauce
- soy granules as mince
- a smaller amount of chicken for meat texture
- canned tomato
- spinach/peas/carrot folded into sauce
- olive oil
- optional cheese if a strong sale makes it worthwhile

The oats / milk / peanut butter / blueberries / flax could become a thick companion pudding or shake during the same meal window. If the one-object rule remains sacred, later tests can try incorporating some of those ingredients into savoury components, but taste wins over philosophical purity.

## Main R&D risk now

**Food volume.**

Cheap protein tends to arrive attached to lots of fibre and water after cooking. 100 g dry lentils + 100 g dry soy + oats + vegetables is a serious amount of food.

The next optimization should therefore track two efficiencies separately:

1. **dollars per target**, and
2. **stomach volume / eating pleasure per target**.

The cheapest mathematical meal is useless if the final third becomes punishment.
