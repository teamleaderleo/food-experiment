# Palatability + Convenience Budget — 2026-09-07

## Why this exists

The project should stop optimizing at the point where another dollar saved makes the food worse, slower, less varied, or more annoying to prepare.

The current practical target is **roughly CAD $10–15/day for normal repeatable food**, with occasional higher-cost days when the upgrade meaningfully improves taste or convenience. The ~$7/day theoretical floor remains a benchmark, not a lifestyle mandate.

## Convenience is a legitimate ingredient

### Frozen aromatics

Frozen diced onion at ~$0.45/100 g is more expensive than whole onions but removes:

- peeling
- chopping
- tears
- leftover half-onion management
- spoilage risk

For repeatable cooking, that can easily be worth the premium.

The same logic should be tested for:

- frozen pepper/onion blends
- frozen spinach
- frozen peas
- frozen garlic/ginger when available at reasonable pricing

### Concentrated flavour bases

Jarred/cubed flavour systems are disproportionately useful because they let the same starch/protein/freezer inventory become different cuisines.

Core candidates:

- pesto
- Japanese curry roux
- Indian curry paste
- doubanjiang / chili-bean paste
- gochujang
- salsa
- soy sauce + rice vinegar
- bouillon / stock concentrate
- Kewpie or other mayonnaise
- hot sauce

The goal is a **flavour library**, not a giant refrigerator full of fragile ingredients.

## Pantry architecture: cheap backbone, premium surface

### Cheap backbone

Keep these economically optimized because they are used in large quantities:

- pasta
- rice
- tortillas / naan when sale-priced
- milk
- eggs
- frozen spinach / peas
- bananas
- tomato concentrate
- iodized salt
- cooking oils
- lentils / beans / small soy extender if tolerated

### Premium surface

Spend more freely on things consumed in small quantities that dominate taste:

- better meat
- parmesan / good melting cheese
- pesto

- fresh herbs
- kimchi / pickles / chutney / salsa
- Kewpie / garlic sauce / tzatziki

- fish / seafood rotation

This is probably the right division for a diet eaten repeatedly.

## Saturated-fat guardrail

The project is a bulk, so high total calories are intentional. That makes fat quality worth watching because saturated fat can quietly become huge when whole milk + cheese + fatty pork/beef + butter/cream all pile into the same day.

Health Canada recommends replacing foods containing mostly saturated fat with foods containing mostly unsaturated fat. WHO recommends keeping saturated fat below 10% of total energy.

At 3,200 kcal/day, 10% of energy is roughly **36 g saturated fat/day**.

R&D implication:

- use canola / olive / sunflower oil for a large share of added calories
- enjoy cheese and richer meat for flavour
- avoid making every calorie upgrade cream/butter/fatty meat simultaneously
- chicken breast/thigh + added unsaturated oil remains a useful comparison against fatty ground meat

This keeps the meal luxurious without letting the bulk become a saturated-fat contest.

## Sodium becomes the other stealth constraint

Health Canada's adult sodium CDRR / daily value is **2,300 mg/day**.

Several flavour tools are very sodium-dense:

- Better Than Bouillon chicken base: ~680 mg sodium per 6 g tsp
- Better Than Bouillon vegetable/beef examples: ~700 mg per tsp
- pesto: Classico lists 600 mg sodium per 60 mL
- curry roux, soy sauce, cheese, MSG blends, kimchi, salsa and processed meat can all add more

The project also intentionally uses iodized salt for iodine.

Therefore:

- season boldly, but count the salty concentrates together
- if using bouillon + soy sauce + cheese + curry paste in one meal, reduce added table salt accordingly
- keep the iodine plug deliberate rather than automatically stacking salt on top of salty sauces

## Energy density + eating rate remain core levers

Recent controlled studies support the idea that **higher energy density and quicker-eating textures can increase energy intake**. A 2025 randomized crossover study manipulated eating rate and energy density and found both relevant to ad libitum energy intake; a 2026 two-week crossover trial showed faster-eating meal textures sustained higher energy intake across days.

For this project that translates to:

- soft starches
- saucy food
- ground/minced proteins
- low dry-chew burden
- moderate liquid calories
- calorie-dense sauces and fats

Liquid carbohydrate also tends to produce less satiety than solid carbohydrate in much of the literature, which supports keeping a drinkable calorie slider available when the bulk gets hard.

## Practical spending tiers

### Floor mode — ~$8–10/day

Use discount meat, generic cheese, simple tomato sauce, frozen veg, basic milk/fruit.

### Normal mode — ~$10–15/day

This should probably become the actual target. Add:

- meat chosen for taste rather than absolute price
- one high-impact sauce/paste
- better cheese
- herbs / pickles / salsa
- convenience vegetables

### Luxury home-food mode — ~$15–20/day

Occasional salmon, steak, good parmesan, pesto, avocado, specialty sauces, bakery bread, etc.

Still usually cheaper than takeout while remaining nutritionally controllable.

## Current thesis

The Ultimate Meal project should optimize **pleasure-adjusted efficiency**, not raw grocery cost.

Once a day is nutritionally sound and lands around $10–15, the next dollar should be spent on whichever of these improves the experience most:

1. better protein taste/texture
2. stronger sauce / flavour identity
3. convenience
4. fresh finishing ingredient
5. variety

The winning diet is one that is cheap enough to feel effortless financially and delicious enough that repetition feels like a privilege instead of discipline.
