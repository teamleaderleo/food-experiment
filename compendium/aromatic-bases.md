# Aromatic Bases — Do the Chopping Once, Then Coast

Use this when the question is:

> **What prepared / blended / frozen base can replace a pile of nightly chopping?**

The project already has a condiment shelf and reusable cooked flavour assets. This file covers the layer in between: **raw or lightly processed aromatic bases** that can be made or bought once and then spooned into many meals.

# 1. Haitian epis

Typical components:

- garlic
- parsley / cilantro
- scallion
- thyme
- bell pepper
- Scotch bonnet / chile
- vinegar / lime
- oil

## What it unlocks

- chicken marinade
- poul ak nwa / Haitian chicken stews
- rice + beans
- soups
- vegetables
- sauces

## Why it fits

Bon Appétit notes that epis can be refrigerated or frozen in portions. That means one blender session can pay off across many meals.

Project use: [`002X`](../experiments/002x-world-tour-round-5.md)

Source: https://www.bonappetit.com/story/epis-seasoning

# 2. Caribbean green seasoning

Typical components vary by household, but often include:

- culantro / cilantro
- scallion
- thyme
- garlic
- onion
- chile
- sometimes ginger / acid

## What it unlocks

- Trinidadian curry chicken
- jerk-adjacent chicken
- fish marinades
- stew bases

## Why it fits

It turns curry chicken from “chop six aromatics” into “spoon in green seasoning + curry powder.”

# 4. Ginger-garlic paste

This is less culturally specific and more like a universal anti-friction tool.

## What it unlocks

- Indian curries
- Sri Lankan curries
- Malaysian curry
- Vietnamese / Chinese marinades
- braises
- stir-fries

## Why it fits

Fresh ginger and garlic remain great when wanted, but paste turns many recipes from chopping tasks into squeeze-and-stir tasks.

Keep fresh garlic for meals where raw garlic flavour is the point; use paste freely when the aromatics are being cooked into sauce.

# 8. Thai curry paste

Technically both spice paste and aromatic base.

## What it unlocks

- red curry
- green curry
- massaman
- sheet-pan curry chicken
- curry noodle soup

## Why it fits

A good paste replaces chile pounding, shallot chopping, lemongrass prep, galangal prep and spice grinding.

This is almost the perfect example of **buy the hard 80%, cook the easy 20%**.

# Make-vs-buy rule

## Buy it when

- the store version tastes good
- chopping is the main friction
- it keeps well
- it unlocks several meals

Examples:

- sofrito
- green seasoning
- ginger-garlic paste
- lemongrass paste
- curry paste

## Make a batch when

- the store version is hard to find / mediocre
- one blender session creates weeks of future meals
- freezing works well

Examples:

- epis
- green seasoning
- chimichurri if the lane sticks

## Make fresh only when the freshness itself is the point

Examples:

- pico de gallo when you actually feel like chopping
- raw herb salads
- certain garnish-heavy sauces

Otherwise buy them.

# Freezer-cube concept

High-leverage experiment:

1. make one batch of epis / green seasoning
2. freeze in small portions or an ice-cube tray
3. transfer frozen portions to a bag
4. pull 1–3 portions when cooking

Potential future freezer:

- epis cubes
- green-seasoning cubes
- ginger-garlic cubes
- pesto cubes
- chimichurri cubes
- tomato/sofrito cubes

This could create a **flavour freezer** analogous to the condiment shelf.

# Current thesis

The best nightly cooking workflow may eventually have four layers:

1. **boring staples** — chicken, fish, tofu, eggs, rice, pasta
2. **dry identity shelf** — spice blends
3. **wet identity shelf** — sauces / pastes / pickles
4. **aromatic freezer** — epis / green seasoning / sofrito-style bases

Then dinner becomes:

> protein + starch + one identity ingredient + one aromatic base → cook mostly unattended

The pantry/freezer does the complexity so the cook does not have to.
