# Experiment 002C: Rabbit Holes That Change the Design

**Date:** 2026-09-07  
**Status:** R&D notes

This pass looks for physiological and culinary constraints that could invalidate the current Ultimate Meal draft even if its price/macros look excellent.

## 1. Fixed 180–220 g protein may be the wrong target

A major meta-analysis of resistance-training studies found the benefit of higher protein intake for fat-free-mass gains plateauing around **1.6 g/kg/day**, with uncertainty around that breakpoint.

For the Ultimate Meal, protein should therefore become **bodyweight-scaled**, not a fixed heroic number.

Working R&D target once bodyweight is known:

- baseline: ~1.6 g/kg/day
- optional high-protein buffer: ~1.8–2.2 g/kg/day if preferred

This could cut a large amount of food volume and cost for a light person while preserving the main bulking objective.

### Important counterpoint: one huge protein meal is not simply wasted

A 2023 tracer study after resistance exercise found **100 g protein produced a larger and more prolonged (>12 h) anabolic response than 25 g**. The old idea that anything above ~20–40 g is automatically oxidized/wasted is too simplistic.

Long-term distribution evidence remains mixed:

- older work found more even protein distribution could increase 24 h muscle protein synthesis and muscle gain compared with highly skewed intake
- a newer 8-week trial in resistance-trained men found **3 vs 5 protein-containing meals produced similar gains** when total daily protein was similar

Practical implication: the Ultimate Meal can remain one *prepared system* while being consumed as **two protein pulses a few hours apart** if that improves comfort and gives repeated eating opportunities.

## 2. The current fortified shake is a lactose bomb

The current 002B shake used:

- 500 mL milk
- 75 g skim milk powder
- 50 g whole milk powder

Canadian Dairy Commission composition data:

- liquid milk: ~4.9% lactose
- skim milk powder: ~49.5–52% lactose
- whole milk powder: ~36–38.5% lactose

That works out to roughly:

- 500 mL milk: ~24.5 g lactose
- 75 g skim powder: ~37–39 g lactose
- 50 g whole powder: ~18–19 g lactose

**Total: roughly 80–83 g lactose in one shake.**

That is the biggest hidden digestive risk in Prototype 002B.

Research on lactose intolerance suggests most affected people tolerate around 12 g lactose at once reasonably well, while symptoms become more common above ~24 g and a 50 g challenge causes symptoms in most lactose-intolerant subjects.

The project should therefore avoid assuming milk powder is a free concentration trick.

### Lactose-control branches

1. ordinary milk + much less milk powder
2. lactose-free milk + whey isolate / milk protein concentrate
3. lactose-free milk + lactase-treated powder mixture
4. yogurt/skyr for part of the dairy, since fermentation can improve tolerance for some people
5. split the dairy load across two drinks

### Milk protein concentrate is conceptually ideal

Canadian Dairy Commission typical MPC-75 composition:

- **75% protein**
- **10.9% lactose**

That is almost exactly the ingredient this project wants: concentrated dairy protein/minerals with far less lactose than skim milk powder. The remaining question is finding a consumer-accessible Canadian source at a sane price.

## 3. Fibre quality matters for throughput, not just fibre grams

The project should stop treating all fibre as equivalent.

Viscous soluble fibres such as:

- psyllium
- guar gum
- pectin
- oat beta-glucan

are particularly effective at increasing viscosity and often satiety. That is useful for weight control and potentially counterproductive for a deliberate high-calorie bulk.

### Throughput rule

Get enough total fibre for the day while avoiding a design dominated by highly viscous fibres.

Prefer the fibre target to come from a mixture of:

- modest lentils
- peas / vegetables
- berries
- some flax
- pasta / grains

Avoid turning the shake into a beta-glucan / psyllium gel.

This makes **reducing oats** in the shake more attractive if viscosity becomes a problem.

## 4. Legumes have a fermentation tax

Legumes and pulses contain galacto-oligosaccharides (GOS), which humans do not digest in the small intestine; gut bacteria ferment them later.

Monash data gives a useful preparation trick: **boiling and draining** or **canning and rinsing** allows water-soluble GOS to leach into the cooking/brine water.

For this experiment:

- red lentils are still excellent for sauce
- if 40–100 g dry lentils causes gas/fullness, test a boiled-and-drained preparation
- canned/drained lentils may beat dry lentils on digestive comfort even if they lose slightly on price
- alpha-galactosidase is an optional experiment if GOS turns out to be the specific failure mode

The rule is tolerance-driven. Do not pay a digestion penalty purely to save twenty cents.

## 5. Particle size is a genuine throughput variable

Older human gastric-emptying work found a homogenized egg meal had a substantially shorter gastric lag phase and half-emptying time than the same meal cut into larger particles.

This supports several current design choices:

- ground pork > chicken chunks for the main
- soy **granules** > soy chunks
- lentils cooked into the sauce
- finely chopped vegetables
- smooth / emulsified sauce
- blended fruit rather than whole frozen berries if chewing becomes the limiting factor

A useful metric to add: **largest recurring particle / chewiness complaint**.

## 6. Fresh pasta may beat meal-prep pasta for this specific goal

Cooling cooked pasta increases resistant starch; recent controlled studies find cooling/reheating can lower the post-meal glucose response compared with freshly cooked pasta.

That is usually discussed as a health advantage.

For a throughput-focused bulk, the trade can reverse:

- fresh pasta = more immediately digestible starch
- cooled/reheated pasta = more resistant starch and potentially more fermentation later

This does **not** mean reheated pasta is bad. It means we should test fresh vs reheated on:

- fullness
- appetite recovery
- gas / fermentation
- convenience

Meal-prep convenience may easily win despite the starch difference.

## 7. Umami is an almost-free palatability multiplier

Umami chemistry gives us a useful culinary shortcut.

Glutamate and 5'-nucleotides such as inosinate/guanylate have a strong sensory synergy. Tomato/cheese/MSG supply glutamate; meat supplies nucleotide-rich savoury notes.

For bolognese, this makes a tiny amount of **MSG** a legitimate R&D ingredient rather than culinary cheating.

Potential palatability stack:

- tomato paste
- browned pork
- mozzarella/parmesan
- MSG
- garlic
- acid at the end
- enough salt

Consumer studies show MSG can increase liking/palatability, including in reduced-salt soups.

This may be one of the cheapest ways to keep the final third of a giant pasta portion enjoyable.

## 8. MCT oil is probably a false lead

MCT oil sounds attractive because it is energy dense and often marketed as rapidly absorbed.

Human work has found MCT-containing meals can increase satiety and sometimes delay gastric emptying compared with long-chain fat controls.

For this project, ordinary olive oil / dairy fat / peanut butter remain better default tools because they are cheaper, taste better in context, and have clearer culinary roles.

## 9. There is a redline turbo-carb branch

If solid-food volume becomes the final bottleneck, glucose polymers become interesting.

Wholesale2Go currently lists maltodextrin at roughly **$90.82 / 25 kg** (~$3.63/kg).

Human gastric-emptying studies show concentrated glucose-polymer solutions can sometimes empty more rapidly than equally concentrated glucose solutions, although energy concentration itself still slows emptying.

This creates an optional branch:

- 50–100 g maltodextrin in a thin drink
- only if ordinary pasta/rice/banana/milk fail the eating-throughput test

It is refined starch, so it should be treated as a **performance tool**, not the nutritional chassis.

## 10. Current design revision

Prototype 002B should become **002B.1** before anyone buys the full ingredient set.

### Savoury

- pasta: 175–225 g dry
- ground pork: 100–150 g
- soy granules: 25–50 g dry
- red lentils: 20–40 g dry
- tomato paste
- modest spinach/peas
- mozzarella: 30–60 g
- olive oil: adjust to calorie target
- MSG + salt + garlic + chili/herbs

### Liquid

Drop the 80 g-lactose super-shake.

Start with something more conservative:

- 500 mL milk or lactose-free milk
- banana
- blueberries
- peanut butter
- **25–40 g skim milk powder max for first test**, or substitute whey/MPC if available
- water/ice to keep viscosity low

Then increase concentration only if digestion remains easy.

## 11. New measurements

Add these to every prototype:

- total protein in g/kg body weight
- grams lactose in each eating episode
- viscous-fibre sources and approximate amounts
- legume dry-equivalent grams
- fresh vs cooled/reheated starch
- active chewing time vs drinking time
- gas/bloating at 2, 4, 6 h
- desire for another meal at 2, 3, 4 h
- final-third palatability score

## Current thesis

The Ultimate Meal probably wins by being **less nutritionally heroic than the spreadsheet wants**.

A slightly more expensive system with:

- bodyweight-appropriate protein
- moderate fibre
- low lactose burden
- fine particle size
- aggressive umami
- a thin calorie-dense drink

is likely to outperform the theoretical $7.50 mega-legume / mega-dairy build in real life.

The true optimum is the cheapest meal that disappears easily and leaves appetite alive.
