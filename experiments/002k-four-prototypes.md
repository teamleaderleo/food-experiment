# Prototype A — Oyakodon bulk bowl

## Why this is so promising

Classic oyakodon is already almost purpose-built for this project: chicken thigh, onion and softly set eggs simmered in dashi/soy/mirin and poured over rice.

A standard Just One Cookbook version uses chicken thigh, onion, dashi, soy, mirin, sugar and 3–4 eggs for two servings, and takes about 25 minutes total.

## Large bulking version

### Main

- white rice — **200 g dry**
- boneless skinless chicken thighs — **250 g raw**
- onion — **100–120 g**, thinly sliced
- eggs — **3 large**
- dashi — **180–220 mL**
- soy sauce — **25–30 mL**
- mirin — **25–30 mL**
- sugar — **10–12 g**
- sake or Shaoxing wine — **10–15 mL**, optional
- neutral oil — **0–8 g**, only if extra calories or browning are desired
- scallion / mitsuba — optional
- shichimi — optional

### Small side

- blanched spinach or bok choy — **75–100 g**
- pickled ginger / cucumber / daikon — **30–50 g**

## Method

1. Start the rice.
2. In a wide skillet, combine dashi, soy, mirin and sugar. Add onion and simmer until soft.
3. Add thin, small pieces of chicken thigh in a single layer. Simmer gently until nearly cooked through.
4. Lightly beat the eggs, preserving some visible white/yolk streaks rather than homogenizing completely.
5. Pour about two-thirds of the egg over the chicken. Cover briefly until softly set.
6. Add the final third and remove from heat while still glossy and barely set.
7. Slide the whole mixture over hot rice with plenty of broth.
8. Add pickles or a sharp side so the giant bowl stays interesting.

## Texture rule

Do not cook the egg firm. The soft egg + broth is the entire throughput advantage.

## Planning macro envelope

Using 200 g dry rice + 250 g thigh + 3 eggs + modest sugar/oil:

- **~1,500 kcal**
- **~80 g protein**

This leaves plenty of room for the standard shake and another smaller eating pulse.

## Convenience version

Use powdered dashi, pre-sliced/frozen onion, boneless thighs and a rice cooker. There is almost nothing left to prep besides cutting chicken.

## A/B tests

1. 200 g vs 170 g dry rice
2. 250 g vs 200 g chicken
3. 2 vs 3 eggs
4. more broth vs tighter sauce
5. thigh vs ground chicken
6. pickled ginger vs quick vinegar cucumber

# Prototype B — Chicken adobo rice

## Why this one deserves repeated testing

The core Filipino adobo pattern is extremely efficient: chicken braised with vinegar, soy sauce, garlic, black pepper and bay leaves, usually served with rice.

The simplest versions need only about 10 minutes of hands-on prep, then the same braising liquid becomes the sauce.

## Large bulking version

### Main

- white rice — **180 g dry**
- boneless skinless chicken thighs — **300 g raw**
- soy sauce — **35–45 mL**
- cane / rice / white vinegar — **35–45 mL**
- garlic — **4–6 cloves**, smashed
- black peppercorns — **1 tsp**, cracked
- bay leaves — **2**
- brown/white sugar — **10–15 g**, optional but useful for balance
- water or low-sodium chicken broth — **100–150 mL**
- canola oil — **0–10 g**, depending whether browning first

### Optional

- 2 eggs, boiled and added late to absorb sauce
- scallion
- lime
- cucumber / pickled pepper side

## Preferred method — brown, braise, reduce

1. Combine soy, garlic, pepper and chicken for a short 20–30 minute marinade when convenient.
2. Brown the chicken in a little oil. This is optional on exhausted nights, but worth testing for flavour.
3. Add reserved marinade, bay leaves, broth/water and vinegar.
4. Simmer gently until the chicken is tender.
5. Remove chicken briefly if needed and reduce the liquid until glossy but still pourable.
6. Return chicken to sauce and serve over rice.

## Sauce rule

Restaurant-style reduction can get sticky. For this project, stop reducing while there is still enough sauce to season a huge bowl of rice.

## Planning macro envelope

With 300 g thigh, 180 g dry rice, 2 eggs, 10 g oil and ~15 g sugar:

- **~1,500 kcal**
- **~80 g protein**

Without the eggs it becomes a slightly lighter main and the eggs can move to another eating pulse.

## Batch advantage

Adobo is a strong 3–4 portion braise candidate. Batch the chicken and sauce; make rice fresh or freeze portions.

## A/B tests

1. equal soy/vinegar vs slightly more vinegar
2. browned vs straight braise
3. 10 g vs 15 g sugar
4. boneless thigh vs bone-in thigh
5. sauce reduced tight vs deliberately loose
6. eggs braised in sauce vs eggs elsewhere

# Prototype C — Macarona béchamel, repeatable-bulk edition

## What the source recipe teaches

A current Little Spice Jar version uses:

- 1 lb lean ground beef
- 14 oz penne
- tomato sauce/paste + warm spices
- a béchamel built from butter, flour, whole milk + half-and-half
- mozzarella

The author explicitly prefers lean beef because the béchamel is already rich and warns that the dish can become heavy if the meat is too fatty.

That points directly at our adaptation: **keep the beef lean, keep the sauce luxurious, and avoid spending all the richness budget on saturated fat.**

## Four-big-portion batch

### Pasta + meat

- penne — **450 g dry**
- 90–95% lean ground beef — **500 g**
- onion — **150 g**, finely chopped
- garlic — **6–10 cloves**
- tomato paste — **30–40 g**
- passata / tomato sauce — **250 g**
- olive or canola oil — **15 g**
- cinnamon — **1/2 tsp**
- allspice — **1/4 tsp**
- nutmeg — tiny pinch
- beef bouillon / stock concentrate — modest amount
- iodized salt as needed
- optional frozen spinach — **100 g**, thawed and squeezed, folded into meat sauce

### Béchamel

- butter — **60 g**
- flour — **60 g**
- whole milk — **900 mL**
- mozzarella — **150 g**
- nutmeg — **1/4 tsp**
- white pepper
- bay leaf — **1**

### Why whole milk only

Half-and-half is delicious, but whole milk already gives a rich béchamel and keeps this batch easier to repeat several times per week if it wins.

## Method

1. Cook penne about 2 minutes short of package doneness.
2. Brown onion, garlic and beef. Fry tomato paste briefly, then add passata, bouillon and warm spices. Simmer until thick but moist.
3. For béchamel, melt butter with bay leaf. Whisk in flour for about a minute.
4. Slowly whisk in milk. Simmer until silky and lightly thickened.
5. Remove bay leaf; season with nutmeg and white pepper. Stir in half the mozzarella.
6. Mix roughly a quarter of the béchamel through the pasta.
7. Layer pasta, meat sauce, remaining béchamel and remaining mozzarella.
8. Bake at about **190°C / 375°F** covered until hot through, then uncover to brown.
9. Rest before cutting, but for throughput testing it may be even better served softer and spoonable rather than waiting for perfect slices.

## Planning macro envelope

Whole tray, generic values:

- **~4,500 kcal**
- **~235–240 g protein**

Divided into 4 large meals:

- **~1,125 kcal**
- **~60 g protein** each

This is lower-calorie per sitting than some flagship rice bowls, which is useful: add garlic bread, extra pasta, a milkshake, or a dessert module according to appetite.

## Convenience trick

Make **two trays** when committing to this dish: bake one, freeze one assembled before baking. If that workflow feels easy, this could become one of the best effort-per-meal recipes in the project.

## A/B tests

1. 500 g beef vs 400 g beef
2. 60 g butter vs 45 g butter + a little olive oil elsewhere
3. 900 mL whole milk vs richer half-and-half version
4. 100 g hidden spinach vs none
5. 150 g vs 200 g mozzarella
6. softer scoopable bake vs firmer sliceable bake

# Prototype D — Chinese eggplant with garlic sauce + pork

## Why this one is unusually good for a bulk

The Woks of Life's current restaurant-style recipe uses Chinese/Japanese eggplant, ground pork, lots of garlic, vinegar, Shaoxing wine, oyster sauce, soy, sesame oil and enough sauce to spoon over rice.

Their updated version specifically avoids deep-frying, sears the eggplant until tender, and still uses a meaningful amount of oil. The base recipe uses about **115 g pork with 450 g eggplant** and intentionally keeps the finished dish saucy.

For us, the obvious adaptation is more pork, a huge rice portion, and the same silky eggplant logic.

## Large bulking version

### Main

- white rice — **180 g dry**
- Chinese/Japanese eggplant — **300–350 g**
- ground pork — **200–225 g**
- neutral/canola oil — **30–35 g**
- garlic — **5–6 cloves**, minced
- ginger — **8–10 g**, minced
- dried chilies — to taste
- scallion — optional

### Sauce

- hot water — **100–120 mL**
- sugar — **10–12 g**
- cornstarch — **8–10 g**
- oyster sauce — **20–25 g**
- rice vinegar — **15 mL**
- Shaoxing wine — **15 mL**
- light soy sauce — **15 mL**
- dark soy — **3–5 mL**
- sesame oil — **5 mL**
- white pepper

## Method

1. Start rice.
2. Mix the sauce first.
3. Cut eggplant into small bite-size pieces.
4. Sear eggplant in oil over medium-high heat until collapsed, glossy and very tender. Add a splash of water and cover briefly if it needs help softening.
5. Remove eggplant; brown pork in the same wok.
6. Add ginger, chili and half the garlic; fry until fragrant.
7. Return eggplant. Add sauce and remaining garlic.
8. Simmer only until glossy and thick enough to coat the food while leaving plenty of sauce for rice.
9. Serve immediately over soft rice.

## Planning macro envelope

With 180 g dry rice, 200 g pork, ~350 g eggplant, 35 g oil and sauce:

- **~1,550–1,650 kcal**
- **~50–55 g protein**
- **~12–13 g fibre**

This is one of the rare large meals where a substantial vegetable portion actually helps texture instead of fighting throughput.

## Protein adjustment

If 50–55 g protein feels too low for the day's layout:

- increase pork to 250 g, or
- add 2 eggs elsewhere, or
- pair the day with a higher-protein dairy eating pulse.

Do **not** automatically add tofu to the bowl unless it improves the meal; the eggplant already creates plenty of food volume.

## A/B tests

1. 300 g vs 350 g eggplant
2. 200 g vs 250 g pork
3. 30 g vs 40 g oil
4. sear only vs sear + brief covered steam
5. 10 g vs 12 g sugar
6. 100 mL vs 140 mL sauce water
