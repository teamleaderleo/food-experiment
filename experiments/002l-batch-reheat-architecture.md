# Experiment 002L: Batch + Reheat Architecture

**Date:** 2026-09-07  
**Status:** workflow design

## Goal

Use a very light form of meal prep:

> **Cook once, eat tonight, refrigerate tomorrow's portion, and freeze only the extra portions that would otherwise sit too long.**

No Sunday assembly line. No wall of identical labeled containers. No requirement to portion every gram in advance.

The project should feel like normal cooking with intentional leftovers.

## Default batch pattern

For most successful dishes, cook roughly **3–4 large servings** at once.

A simple default:

- serving 1: eat immediately
- serving 2: refrigerator for tomorrow
- serving 3: refrigerator if likely to be eaten within the next couple of days
- serving 4: freeze as insurance / future low-effort dinner

If the meal is so good that two refrigerated leftovers will obviously disappear quickly, freezing is optional.

## Why this fits the project

The biggest convenience gain does not come from pre-portioning every ingredient. It comes from avoiding repeated:

- chopping
- browning meat
- opening cans/jars
- washing pans
- measuring sauces
- waiting for braises

A 35-minute recipe that produces three future meals can have a much lower average effort than a nominally 15-minute recipe cooked from scratch every day.

## Separate only when quality earns it

Do **not** automatically create six containers for one meal.

Store the whole cooked dish together when it reheats well that way:

- adobo
- rou zao fan
- Japanese curry
- butter-chicken-style curry
- mapo tofu sauce
- Chinese eggplant + pork
- cottage pie
- macarona béchamel
- chili / keema / ragù

Keep components separate only when texture benefits:

- rice from especially wet sauces if it would become mushy
- noodles from sesame/peanut sauces if they would absorb everything overnight
- fresh herbs
- pickles / kimchi / cucumber
- Kewpie / toum / tahini finishers
- crispy fried shallots
- toasted nuts
- fresh red pepper / acidic salad pieces

The rule is **minimum number of containers compatible with a good reheated meal**.

## Dish-specific strategy

### Rou zao fan

Batch the pork braise and eggs. Rice can be cooked fresh, refrigerated separately, or frozen in portions.

Very strong batch candidate because the braise reheats beautifully and may taste even more integrated the next day.

### Chicken adobo

Batch chicken directly in its sauce. Store together. Make enough sauce that reheating does not dry it out.

### Japanese curry / hayashi rice

Batch sauce/protein/vegetables. Keep rice separate for easier texture control.

### Oyakodon

Better as a quick fresh meal than a huge batch because softly set egg loses some magic after reheating.

A useful compromise is to prep the chicken/onion/dashi base for 2–3 meals, then add fresh egg when reheating/cooking each portion.

### Dan dan noodles

Batch pork + ya cai topping and premix sauce base. Cook noodles fresh when possible.

This gives nearly all the convenience without storing finished noodles that may turn gummy or dry.

### Shawarma chicken

Two good workflows:

1. cook 2–3 servings of thighs and refrigerate leftovers
2. freeze raw chicken already divided in marinade, then air-fry fresh later

Keep toum/tahini/pickles separate.

### Macarona béchamel / cottage pie

These justify classic meal-prep behavior because they are literally batch bakes.

Bake one tray, cut/scoop portions as needed. Freeze surplus portions once cool.

### Chinese eggplant + pork

Store the cooked dish together. Keep rice separate. Reheat gently with a splash of water so the sauce loosens again.

### Fried rice

This is a **leftover conversion meal** rather than a primary batch recipe. Refrigerated rice becomes an ingredient for the next dinner.

## The 48-hour philosophy

The most pleasant rhythm may be:

- cook a dish tonight
- deliberately make enough for tomorrow
- maybe make one extra portion beyond that
- decide tomorrow whether that extra portion gets eaten soon or frozen

This keeps the fridge dynamic instead of turning food into a long-term inventory-management project.

## Freezer as pressure relief, not the main event

Freezing is useful for:

- fourth portions
- batch bakes
- braises/curries when a large batch was effortless
- raw marinated chicken
- emergency dinners

Freezing is less attractive for:

- delicate egg dishes
- already-dressed noodles
- fresh herbs / crunchy toppings
- meals that are trivially quick to make fresh

The freezer's job is to prevent waste and create occasional zero-cook nights, not to make every dinner taste thawed.

## Food-safety guardrails

Health Canada guidance supports the simple workflow:

- refrigerate or freeze leftovers within about **2 hours**
- use **shallow containers** so large batches cool quickly
- keep the refrigerator at **4°C / 40°F or below**
- refrigerated cooked leftovers are generally a **2–4 day** food; Health Canada's leftover-specific page recommends **2–3 days**, while its broader storage tables list many cooked dishes at **3–4 days**
- reheat leftovers to **74°C / 165°F**
- generally avoid repeatedly reheating the same batch; reheat only the portion being eaten

Cooked rice deserves prompt cooling too. Canadian food-safety guidance specifically notes that cooked rice can support *Bacillus cereus* growth if it sits warm too long; shallow storage and prompt refrigeration are the answer.

## Best batch sizes by meal type

### 2 servings

Use when freshness is central:

- oyakodon
- soboro don
- delicate seafood dishes
- pesto pasta

### 3–4 servings

Default sweet spot:

- rou zao fan
- adobo
- curry
- shawarma chicken
- keema
- ragù
- eggplant + pork
- mapo sauce

### 4–8 servings

Worth it when the dish is designed for a tray/pot:

- macarona béchamel
- cottage pie
- chili
- large braise
- large curry if freezer space is available

## Average-effort metric

Add one new project metric:

> **active prep minutes per meal actually obtained**

Example:

- 32 active minutes to make 4 dinners = **8 active minutes per dinner**

That number is more useful than the recipe's headline cook time.

Also record:

- first-night quality
- next-day quality
- frozen/reheated quality if tested
- number of containers dirtied
- number of pans dirtied
- whether the reheated version needs a fresh finishing ingredient

## Fresh-finisher trick

A reheated meal can feel newly cooked if one 20-second addition changes it:

- squeeze of lime/lemon
- chopped scallion/cilantro
- fresh black pepper
- chili crisp
- Kewpie
- toum
- tahini
- pickles
- grated parmesan
- fried shallots

This may be the highest-return meal-prep technique in the project: **batch the boring 95%, refresh the final 5% at serving time.**

## Current thesis

The best version of meal prep for this project is deliberately informal:

> **Cook a good dinner in a slightly stupid quantity. Eat it. Put tomorrow's food in the fridge. Freeze only what is clearly surplus. Add one fresh finishing move when reheating.**

That captures most of the labor savings of formal meal prep without making eating feel like inventory management.
